## Bottom line

Whether Spinosaurus was an aquatic pursuit predator remains highly contested, with evidence pointing toward different ecological roles. The aquatic pursuit hypothesis is supported by a fin-like tail capable of significant thrust, bone compactness suggesting diving capabilities, and neuroanatomy indicating eye-guided pursuit of fish. However, these claims are strongly challenged by evidence regarding buoyancy and stability; models suggest Spinosaurus was too buoyant to submerge and laterally unstable, making it a slow, awkward swimmer. Additionally, critics argue that bone compactness may result from body mass or preservation rather than diving adaptations.

Stronger support exists for the shoreline generalist model. Isotopic analyses provide robust evidence that Spinosaurus was a semi-aquatic predator with a primarily piscivorous diet, spending much of its time in water. While jaw mechanics and tooth histology confirm piscivory, they do not distinguish between pursuit and shoreline hunting. The key disagreement centers on whether its morphology enabled efficient underwater pursuit or if its physical parameters limited it to a semi-aquatic, shoreline-based existence.

## Hypotheses considered

- `aquatic_pursuit`: Spinosaurus was a specialized aquatic predator capable of tail-propelled swimming and pursuit of prey underwater.
- `shoreline_generalist`: Spinosaurus was a semi-aquatic or shoreline generalist that hunted primarily in shallow water or on land.
- `terrestrial_predator`: Spinosaurus was primarily terrestrial and lacked the buoyancy or stability required for efficient aquatic pursuit.

### SQ1. What does the morphology of the Spinosaurus tail and hindlimbs suggest about its locomotion?

**Verdict: contested.** The evidence is highly contested among primary sources. Ibrahim et al. (2020) argue that the fin-like tail and reduced hindlimbs indicate a specialized aquatic pursuit predator. In contrast, Hone & Holtz (2021) suggest the tail lacks necessary muscle attachments and the hindlimbs are not uniquely aquatic, supporting a shoreline generalist model. Henderson (2018) argues that the general Tetanuran morphology, including stiffened tails and parasagittal limbs, makes tail-propelled swimming improbable, supporting a terrestrial predator model.

The locomotion of Spinosaurus is contested. Some evidence suggests it was a specialized aquatic predator; its tail featured tall neural spines and elongate chevrons forming a flexible, fin-like organ capable of significant lateral movement [ibrahim2020_nature, p.2]. This morphology, combined with reduced zygapophyses in the distal tail, may have generated over eight times the thrust of other theropod tails [ibrahim2020_nature, p.4][ibrahim2020_nature, p.6], making it more efficient in water than terrestrial dinosaurs [ibrahim2020_nature, p.2]. Additionally, reduced hindlimbs and wide feet are cited as semi-aquatic adaptations [ibrahim2020_nature, p.3]. 

Conversely, others argue it was a shoreline generalist, noting that the tail lacks the rugose muscle attachments seen in crocodilians [honeholtz2021_pe, p.12] and that its swimming performance was lower than that of crocodiles and newts [honeholtz2021_pe, p.13]. They suggest flattened unguals may result from large size rather than aquatic adaptation [honeholtz2021_pe, p.13], while a robust fourth trochanter on the femur suggests terrestrial capability [honeholtz2021_pe, p.14]. Finally, a terrestrial predator model posits that as a Tetanuran, Spinosaurus likely had a stiffened tail [henderson2018_peerj, p.3] and parasagittal hind limbs poorly suited for aquatic environments [henderson2018_peerj, p.3], lacking the hip and spinal adaptations found in semi-aquatic crocodilians [henderson2018_peerj, p.3].
